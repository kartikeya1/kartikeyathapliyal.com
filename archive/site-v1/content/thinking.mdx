## How I think

I think in systems. When a product feels broken, the screen is rarely where it broke. It broke upstream — in an incentive, a handoff between teams, or a decision nobody wrote down. So I start by mapping the system: who touches this, what each of them optimizes for, and where information gets lost in between. The interface is usually the last thing I fix.

I also believe most complexity is imported, not essential. Someone chose it, usually for reasons that were good at the time and have since expired. A large part of product work is finding complexity that has outlived its reason and quietly retiring it.

## The problems that excite me

Ecosystems. Products with more than two sides — platforms, integrations, partnerships — where your users include other companies' engineers and your constraints include other companies' roadmaps. These problems don't yield to a better screen. They yield to better primitives, better contracts, and better defaults.

The test I apply to any new problem: if solving it well would make ten future problems disappear, I want it.

## Why product

Because the interesting failures happen between disciplines. Engineering optimizes for correctness, design for clarity, business for growth — and all three can succeed locally while the product fails. Product management, done honestly, is owning the space in between: making sure the thing being built is worth building, buildable, and understood the same way by everyone building it.

## How I work

I write things down. A decision that isn't written down will be made again, worse, by someone with less context. Writing is also how I find out whether I actually understand something — vague thinking survives meetings but not paragraphs.

I prototype early. A working artifact ends arguments that documents only start. And I keep loops short: decide what would prove us wrong, ship the smallest thing that tests it, then look honestly at what happened.

## Principles

- **Simplicity is a feature.** It has a real cost, and it is usually worth paying.
- **Design for the second user.** Anything built for one team, one campaign, or one partner will be asked to serve ten. The second user arrives sooner than you think.
- **Decisions decay.** Record them with their reasons, so future people can tell when the reasons have expired.
- **Reversible decisions fast, irreversible decisions slowly.** Most decisions are the first kind pretending to be the second.
- **Legibility over cleverness.** If the team can't explain how something works, they can't fix it, extend it, or trust it.

## On ambiguity

My first move is to make ambiguity smaller, not to resolve it. Most of it dissolves into two lists: decisions nobody has made, and questions nobody has tested. Undecided things need an owner and a date. Untested things need the cheapest experiment that could change our minds. What remains — genuine uncertainty — is almost always smaller than it first looked, and you can usually buy it down cheaply.

The failure mode I guard against is treating ambiguity as a reason to wait. It is usually a reason to write.

## Working with engineers

I bring problems, not tickets. Engineers do their best work when they own the how and co-own the why, so my job is context, constraints, and a crisp definition of done — then staying reachable instead of hovering.

I read the code sometimes. Not to review it, but because architecture shapes product: it decides what is cheap, what is expensive, and what becomes possible next quarter. And I respect estimates by respecting scope — when timelines slip, I cut scope, not corners.

## Evaluating tradeoffs

Tradeoffs are only painful when goals are secret. So the first step is making the currency explicit: what are we actually optimizing for right now, and what are we knowingly letting get worse? Then I classify by reversibility, and I write down what we chose *not* to do next to what we chose.

A tradeoff you didn't write down is a surprise you scheduled for later.
