## Overview

At smallcase I was handed a problem statement one sentence long. No brief, no target user, no definition of done. This case study is about the work between that sentence and a shipped product: interviewing stakeholders to find the actual constraint, writing a problem statement people could disagree with productively, cutting scope to a first version that tested the riskiest assumption, and sequencing engineering so value shipped before the full vision was built.

## Problem

The sentence I was given described a symptom, not a problem. It was easy to agree with, which was exactly the issue. Everyone who repeated it meant something slightly different, and each interpretation implied a different product. Before anything could be built, someone had to establish what we were solving, for whom, and what evidence would tell us we were done. That was my job for the first few weeks.

## Constraints

Three constraints shaped everything. Engineering capacity was fixed and shared with other commitments, so the plan had to fit the team we had, not the team we wanted. The product touched flows that partner brokers and compliance cared about, which put a floor on review time regardless of how fast we moved. And the window to ship was set by external dates we did not control.

## Thinking

My first move was interviews, not documents. I spoke with every stakeholder who had an opinion on the one-sentence brief and asked what would have to be true for them to call it a success. The answers did not agree. That disagreement was the useful output: it located the actual constraint, which was not the one named in the original sentence.

I then wrote a problem statement specific enough to be wrong. Vague framings get nodding agreement and stall later. A sharp one forces the argument early, when changing course is cheap. Two rounds of pointed objections later, we had a framing everyone had actively agreed to rather than passively accepted.

## Process

With the problem fixed, I listed our assumptions and ranked them by how expensive each would be if false. The riskiest one became the test for the first version. Everything that did not bear on that assumption was cut, written down, and dated, so the cuts read as sequencing decisions rather than losses. Saying no is easier when the no comes with a when.

## Design

The first version was deliberately narrow. We designed the smallest complete experience that could carry the core promise of the product, not a thinner copy of the full vision. Working with design, I pushed for one primary flow done properly over three flows done partially, because a half-built flow teaches you nothing about whether the idea works.

## Execution

I sequenced the engineering plan around a simple rule: every milestone had to leave something usable behind. Internal releases came first, then a limited release to a small group, then general availability. Each stage was a checkpoint where we could stop, learn, or reorder what came next. The full vision stayed on the roadmap, but nothing depended on all of it landing at once.

## Outcome

The product shipped inside the window, and the first version answered the question it was built to answer. The riskiest assumption held, which unlocked confidence and budget for the next phases. Launch was not the end of the work. I treated it as the start of the argument: the point where real usage began to confirm or correct the framing we had committed to.

## Learnings

The scarce skill was not generating ideas but subtracting them. Most of the value came from three decisions: locating the real constraint before writing anything, framing the problem sharply enough to invite disagreement, and cutting scope in a way that preserved a complete experience. If I ran this again, I would compress the interview phase; the signal arrived earlier than I trusted it.
